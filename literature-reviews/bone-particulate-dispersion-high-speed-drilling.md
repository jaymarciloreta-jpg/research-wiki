# Dispersion of Bone Particulate and Aerosol from High-Speed Surgical Drilling

> **Scope.** A review of the peer-reviewed literature on how bone particulate
> ("bone dust"), debris, and aggregate are generated and dispersed when
> high-speed rotary instruments (drills, burrs, saws) cut bone during surgery —
> particle characteristics, spread distance, infection/aerosol risk, the drill
> parameters that drive dispersion, thermal effects, biological consequences,
> and mitigation. Covers orthopedic, spinal, neurosurgical, otologic/skull-base
> (ENT), oral/maxillofacial, and dental contexts.
>
> **Status:** Draft for review. Every quantitative claim is attributed to a
> verified source in [References](#references). A few figures that could not be
> re-confirmed against full text (NCBI/PMC was access-blocked during research)
> are flagged inline as *[figure per source]*.

## 1. Summary

High-speed surgical drills and burrs (typically ~12,000–80,000+ RPM) do not
produce a tidy pile of shavings. They eject a heterogeneous cloud of bone dust,
blood, marrow, irrigation droplets, and soft-tissue debris that travels across
and well beyond the operative field. The literature is consistent on several
points:

- Bone particulate spans **six orders of magnitude in size** and is
  **predominantly irregular/non-spherical** — jagged fragments, not droplets.
- Debris and aerosol routinely **reach the surgeon's face**, and measurable
  contamination is detectable **1.5–2 m** from the burr, with a steep near-field
  gradient.
- The substrate being drilled can carry pathogens, and bone drilling is now
  classed as a high-risk **aerosol-generating procedure (AGP)**.
- **Irrigation is the single most protective parameter** against both thermal
  injury and fine dust, and **combined irrigation + suction + respiratory
  filtration cuts airborne particulate by >99%**.

The dual nature of bone debris recurs throughout: collected bone dust is a
useful autograft, yet stray particulate causes thermal necrosis, foreign-body
reaction, ectopic bone, and (in tumor surgery) a plausible seeding mechanism.

## 2. Particle characteristics and dispersion

**Size and morphology.** The most detailed orthopedic characterization is a
laboratory worst-case bovine-bone machining simulation by **Putzer et al.
(2022, *International Orthopaedics*)** [[2]](#ref2), which found particle
cross-sectional areas spanning **0.006–4 mm² (median 0.125 mm²)** with **89% of
particles non-circular** — i.e., bone dust is dominated by jagged, irregular
fragments rather than spherical droplets. The authors concluded high-speed bone
machining is "potentially harmful by pollution" and argued for containment.

In otologic surgery, **Pirola et al. (2021, *European Archives of
Oto-Rhino-Laryngology*)** [[6]](#ref6) used methylene-blue-dyed irrigation on a
cadaveric mastoidectomy/translabyrinthine model and recovered droplets ranging
from **2 µm to 2.6 cm**, with dyed droplets visualized **150 cm** from the
emission focus. They stressed that sub-2 µm particles can stay airborne for
hours and may leave theater via positive-pressure ventilation. Mastoid drilling
is repeatedly identified as producing abundant **1–10 µm** aerosol.

**Spread distance.** Across mastoidectomy studies, the systematic review by
**Favre et al. (2021, *Cureus*)** [[9]](#ref9) reports a measured spread range of
**30–208 cm**, with fluorescent-droplet methods detecting longer distances than
optical particle counters — suggesting optical methods *underestimate* true
reach. In high-speed bone burring with external irrigation, **Putzer et al.
(2021, *Journal of Microbiological Methods*)** [[1]](#ref1) found
bacteria-enriched irrigation deposited most heavily **perpendicular to the burr
rotation axis, ~0.5 m from the burr at ~1.4 m height**, with colony-forming
units detectable throughout the room. A concentric-circle analysis in the tumor
study by **Wang et al. (2020, *BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders*)** [[3]](#ref3)
quantified the steep near-field gradient: **72.6% ± 5.8% of contamination within
10 cm, 22.1% ± 4.2% at 20 cm, and 5.4% ± 1.5% at 30 cm** — meaningful far-field
deposition despite the concentration near the burr.

**Reaching the surgeon.** **Cook et al. (2019, *International Journal of Spine
Surgery*)** [[4]](#ref4) inspected face shields after 46 consecutive spine
surgeries (staff in space-suit PPE): **surgeon face-shield debris in 38/46 (83%)
cases**, first assistant 35%, scrub tech 0%. Procedure-specific rates hit **100%
for TLIF and open laminectomy/fusion and 93% for ACDF** — the procedures most
reliant on high-speed burring and osteotomy. That burr debris routinely reaches
the surgeon's face is among the better-established findings.

## 3. Aerosol-generating procedures and infection risk

The COVID-19 pandemic reframed bone drilling as a high-risk AGP.
**Hamilton et al. (2023, *Bone & Joint Research*)** [[7]](#ref7) showed, in a
sterile laminar-flow environment, that **bone sawing and diathermy are top-tier
aerosol generators** even under laminar flow.

**The substrate can carry pathogen.** A prospective study, *Detection of
SARS-CoV-2 in Cancellous Bone…* (2022, *Int. J. Environmental Research and Public
Health*) [[18]](#ref18), found **29/30 (96%)** cancellous-bone samples RT-PCR
positive for SARS-CoV-2, including in convalescent patients who had already
cleared nasopharyngeal infection — implying that drilling bone can aerosolize
viral material.

**Documented harm to staff.** The YO-IFOS international registry, **Sowerby et
al. (2020, *International Forum of Allergy & Rhinology*)** [[17]](#ref17),
identified 361 otolaryngologists with COVID-19 (325 analyzed) and **24 deaths**,
with several instructional cases linked to AGPs including mastoidectomy and
translabyrinthine resection. This is associational, not causal, but it is the
strongest real-world signal.

**Important caveat — detection ≠ transmission.** Surgical-smoke reviews
(**Mowbray et al., 2020, *British Journal of Surgery*** [[10]](#ref10)) establish
that viral material (HPV, HBV, HIV proviral DNA) has been detected in plume, but
**HPV is the only virus with documented clinical transmission to staff via
plume**. Most plume evidence concerns electrocautery/laser rather than bone
drilling, so infection risk *specifically via bone aerosol* remains partly
extrapolated. Treat "pathogen DNA detected" and "viable airborne transmission
demonstrated" as distinct claims.

## 4. Drill parameters that drive dispersion

- **Burr type/size:** Cutting (fluted) burrs spread more debris than diamond
  burrs, and smaller burrs reduce contamination (mastoidectomy and spatial
  studies [[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)).
- **Irrigation:** Double-edged. Continuous saline cools bone and suppresses fine
  dust, but in a bloody field it can atomize blood/fluid into more droplets
  [[6]](#ref6). Net effect is strongly protective when paired with suction
  (see §7).
- **Rotational speed (RPM):** Higher RPM raises both heat and particulate
  generation; lower speed with irrigation minimizes both [[12]](#ref12).
- **Feed force/rate:** Higher feed rates shorten dwell time (less heat) but alter
  chip size. The direct link from feed force to *airborne* dispersion is thinly
  studied — an evidence gap.

## 5. Thermal effects

Thermal osteonecrosis is one of the best-established topics in this field. The
classic threshold from **Eriksson & Albrektsson (1983, *J. Prosthetic
Dentistry*)** [[11]](#ref11) is **47 °C sustained for ~1 minute** for irreversible
bone necrosis; cell death accelerates near **50 °C (~30 s)** and is immediate
near **70 °C** (reviewed by **Timon & Keady, 2019, *Cureus*** [[12]](#ref12)).
Experimentally, dry drilling can exceed threshold (temperatures into the
50s °C with larger drills at higher speeds), whereas **external irrigation keeps
drilling temperatures in roughly the low-30s °C** *[figure per source]*, well
below threshold, and is the single most important protective factor (**Woods et
al., 2022, *Iowa Orthopaedic Journal*** [[13]](#ref13)).

## 6. Biological and clinical consequences

**Bone dust as graft (useful).** Suction-filter–collected debris retains viable
osteogenic bone — **Patel et al. (2009, *Orthopedics*)** [[14]](#ref14) confirmed
viable bone in high-speed burr shavings collected during spinal decompression —
and such debris is used as autograft.

**Stray debris (harmful).** Loose bone particulate can cause **ectopic/heterotopic
bone formation** and **foreign-body / inflammatory reactions** (mononuclear
phagocytes fusing into multinucleated giant cells around bone particles).

**Tumor seeding.** **Wang et al. (2020, *BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders*)**
[[3]](#ref3) used luminescent cement to mimic residual tumor in a porcine model
and found high-speed burring produced **spray reaching up to 20 cm**. A handpiece
cover did **not** reduce spray within the surgical field (and increased deposition
within 10 cm), though it reduced contamination of the surgeon's glove and could
limit spread beyond 20 cm — a plausible, if pilot-level, mechanism for local
recurrence after burr-assisted curettage.

**Staff exposure.** Ocular and respiratory exposure is a recognized route; bone
dust is not reliably stopped by standard surgical masks, whereas N95/FFP3
respirators substantially reduce — but do not eliminate — particulate inhalation
(see §7).

## 7. Mitigation

- **Combined irrigation + suction + respiratory filtration is the strongest
  evidence-based strategy.** **Schramm et al. (2021, *Acta Neurochirurgica*)**
  [[5]](#ref5) reported that, for drilling, suction, FFP3 filtration, and washing
  each helped, but **combining irrigation with filtration reduced particulate
  aerosol by 97.38% to >99.9%** — though no single measure returned aerosol fully
  to baseline.
- **Barrier drapes / enclosures.** **Chen et al. (2020, *Otology &
  Neurotology*)** [[8]](#ref8) showed a microscope drape effectively contained
  fine mastoidectomy aerosol, and delaying drape removal reduced release. Custom
  enclosed barriers (e.g., the redesigned "OtoTent2") can keep particle density
  near baseline. **Cottrell et al. (2020, *J. Otolaryngology–HNS*)** [[15]](#ref15)
  described an operative barrier system for skull-base/mastoid surgery.
- **Systematic-review support.** **Favre et al. (2021, *Cureus*)** [[9]](#ref9):
  of 20 articles screened, 6 met inclusion (5 cadaver, 1 live human); barriers,
  microscope use, and burr selection all reduced dispersion across a 30–208 cm
  spread range.
- **Dental analogues.** **Al-yaseen et al. (2022, *BDJ Open*)** [[16]](#ref16)
  reviewed rotary-handpiece aerosol/splatter; high-volume suction reduces
  aerosol/splatter by >90%, a transferable lesson for bone work.
- **PPE.** For high-risk bone drilling: N95/FFP3, goggles **and** face shield,
  gown, double gloves. Space-suit systems are debated given the 83% face-shield
  contamination data [[4]](#ref4).

## 8. Benchtop & surrogate models (for device development)

Because this wiki supports device/innovation work, the *methods* used to
quantify dispersion are as relevant as the findings. Validated surrogates in the
literature include:

- **Real bone under worst-case machining** (bovine/porcine), with image analysis
  of collected particles for size/morphology distributions [[2]](#ref2).
- **Bacteria-enriched irrigation** collected on agar/Petri-dish arrays to map
  spatial deposition and CFU gradients [[1]](#ref1).
- **Dyed irrigation** (methylene blue) or **fluorescent droplets** photographed
  against gridded backdrops to measure spread distance — fluorescent methods
  outperform optical counters for far-field spread [[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9).
- **Luminescent cement** as a tracer for residual-material spray [[3]](#ref3).
- **Cascade impactors / optical particle spectrometers** for size-resolved
  airborne fractions [[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8).

These are better characterized for *dust/aerosol metrics* than a whole-egg
surrogate, which models a sealed shell rather than the particle-size and
spread-distance behavior of drilled bone. A bone-mimicking machinable substrate
(e.g., synthetic cortical/cancellous test blocks) combined with a fluorescent or
bacterial tracer would map more directly onto the metrics above.

## 9. Evidence quality

- **Well established:** the 47 °C/1-min necrosis threshold and the protective
  role of irrigation; bone drilling produces wide-spectrum, irregular
  particulate; debris reaches the surgeon's face; combined
  irrigation+suction+respirator dramatically cuts aerosol.
- **Reasonably supported:** spread to ~1.5–2 m; barriers reduce dispersion;
  viral RNA present in bone/middle-ear tissue.
- **Emerging / contested / thin:** real-world transmission *specifically via bone
  aerosol* (largely extrapolated from plume/AGP literature; HPV is the only
  plume-transmitted virus); burr-spray tumor seeding (single pilot animal study);
  quantitative effect of feed force on airborne dispersion; comparative efficacy
  of dedicated bone-debris capture devices.

## 10. Open questions / research gaps

1. No standardized metric or benchtop protocol for "bone-dust dispersion" — size
   distribution, spread distance, and airborne fraction are measured
   inconsistently across studies.
2. Sparse direct data linking **feed force and burr geometry** to *airborne*
   particle spread (most parameter work targets temperature, not dispersion).
3. Limited comparative clinical efficacy data for **dedicated debris-capture /
   shroud devices** as opposed to drapes and suction.
4. Infection risk *specifically attributable to bone aerosol* (vs. electrocautery
   plume) is under-quantified.
